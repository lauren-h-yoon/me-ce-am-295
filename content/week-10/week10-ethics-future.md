**Part I — AI and the Reproducibility Crisis**

Science is grappling with a reproducibility crisis, and ill-informed use of machine learning is driving a deluge of unreliable research. A prime example occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic: numerous papers claimed AI could diagnose the virus from chest X-rays with near-perfect accuracy. Subsequent analysis revealed severe data leakage — the models had learned to recognize the fonts and text-overlay positioning of the hospitals that provided positive cases, not the pathology. Kapoor and Narayanan identified data-leakage problems affecting hundreds of machine-learning papers across 17 scientific fields.

AI agents can also help solve the crisis: autonomous agents can write data-analysis pipelines, containerize execution environments, and generate version-controlled documentation so that every step from raw data to final figure is reproducible — exactly the standard your capstone repository is graded against.

**Part II — The Regulatory Landscape**

As AI agents draft code and perform analysis, who is responsible when a design fails? Professional-engineering guidance (NSPE) is unambiguous: AI use does not change a licensed engineer’s legal responsibilities — engineers must exercise responsible charge and validate all outputs, whether generated by a junior engineer or an AI system. The EU AI Act classifies AI used in critical infrastructure as high-risk, requiring human oversight, logging, and certification. For research settings, the same principles surface as research integrity, export control on models and data, and the physical safety of autonomous laboratories (Week 8): AI agents are tools, not licensed professionals.

**Part III — Authorship and Intellectual Property**

Can an AI agent hold a patent for a composite material it discovered? US courts answered no: in Thaler v. Vidal (Fed. Cir. 2022; cert. denied 2023), the court held that the Patent Act requires inventors to be human beings. The US Copyright Office likewise requires sufficient human authorship for copyright registration of AI-assisted works. Major journals, including Nature and Science, have updated editorial policies: LLMs cannot be credited as authors because they cannot take legal or ethical responsibility for the work. This is the reason for the course’s AI Use Declaration policy: transparency about tool use, with responsibility remaining human.

**Part IV — The Future of Human–AI Collaboration**

In 2025, Stanford hosted Agents4Science, the first conference where LLM agents served as authors and peer reviewers alongside humans. The experiment showed that agents can synthesize literature and draft reasonable hypotheses but still lack deep domain intuition and true novelty generation. For the engineers completing this course, the role is shifting: less time meshing finite-element models and writing boilerplate, more time acting as system orchestrators — defining physical constraints, engineering robust multi-agent prompts, validating AI-generated outputs, and exercising the professional judgment no algorithm can replicate.

**Course Synthesis**

The ten-week arc: LLM foundations (Weeks 1–2) → agents and tools (Week 3) → grounded knowledge with RAG (Week 4) → physics-informed surrogates (Weeks 5–6) → agents that write and debug simulation code (Week 7) → closed-loop discovery (Week 8) → evidence of reliability (Week 9) → professional responsibility (Week 10). The capstone integrates the entire stack.
